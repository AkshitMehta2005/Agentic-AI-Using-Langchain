What is Agentic AI?

The working definition used in the lecture
“Agentic AI is a type of AI that can take up a task and goal from a user and then work towards completing it on its own with minimal human guidance. It plans, takes action, adapts to changes, and seeks help only when necessary.”

01

What agentic AI actually is

Forget the buzzword for a second. The lecture frames agentic AI as a software paradigm, not a model feature.

You hand the system a goal. The system works out how to achieve it: it plans, executes, and adjusts. The planning and the execution both belong to the system, not to you. Human involvement drops to a minimum — and shows up only where it matters.

Given by you

The goal and its constraints

“Hire a backend engineer, 2–4 years experience, remote.” That is the entire input. Everything downstream is the agent's problem.

Owned by the agent

Planning, action, adaptation

Decompose the goal, choose tools, execute step by step, notice when something breaks, and re-plan without being told.

Why it feels different: with a normal chatbot you drive every turn. With an agentic system you describe an outcome once and monitor progress — the loop between “what to do next” and “do it” closes inside the software.

The Goa test (the intuition pump from the lecture)

Travelling to Goa involves dozens of decisions: fix dates, pick transport, shortlist hotels, plan sightseeing, decide what to eat. Watch how the work splits in each paradigm.

With a generative AI chatbotWith an agentic AI system
You ask: “Best way to get to Goa on the 15th?” → it answers exactly that. You say: “I need to be in Goa between these dates.” → it works out the best route itself.
Then you ask about hotels in that duration → it answers that. It comes back with hotel recommendations, unprompted.
Then you ask where to visit given the weather → it answers that. It hands you a full itinerary aligned to your dates.
You hold the thread across every step. The system holds the thread and drives the sequence.
02

Reactive vs. agentic

The single sharpest distinction in the video: a chatbot reacts, an agent initiates.

DimensionGenerative AI chatbot (reactive)Agentic AI system (proactive)
InitiativeWaits for your next messageActs on its own once the goal is set
Scope per turnOne question, one to-the-point answerWhole task, end to end
PlanningNone — you sequence the stepsBuilds and revises the plan itself
MonitoringYou check on progress and report backIt watches the environment continuously
When things breakYou notice, then ask for alternativesIt notices and adapts — or escalates to you
Human effortHigh: a prompt per decisionLow: goal setting, approvals, exceptions

Note the nuance: “proactive” does not mean “uncontrolled”. Agentic systems still ask for permission at risky checkpoints — that is a designed feature, covered under autonomy controls.

03

The AI recruiter walkthrough

One concrete scenario carries the whole lecture. You are an HR recruiter, and your company has handed you an agentic hiring assistant. The task: hire one backend engineer, remote, 2–4 years of experience.

Watch for four things as you read: the plan it proposes, the tools it reaches for, the moment it notices a problem, and the moments it stops to ask you. Those four are the entire rest of the video in miniature.

Goal intake

“I want to hire a backend engineer — remote, 2–4 years experience,” plus a few constraints. No process, no steps, no tool instructions.

Agent interprets the goal

It proposes its own plan

Draft a JD → post it on the best job platforms → monitor applications → screen candidates → schedule interviews → send offer letters → start onboarding for whoever accepts.

Agent plans · You have not specified any of this sequence

JD drafted from company documents

It reads internal docs to work out which technologies the role needs, what the company pays at that experience band, and what the responsibilities are. Then it shows you the draft: “anything to change?”

Human checkpoint You approve the JD

Posted — through tools, not chat

It asks to publish on the relevant platforms, then uses LinkedIn and job-board APIs to post. It notifies you that the job is live and that monitoring has begun.

Tools LinkedIn API · job-board API

It catches a bad outcome before you do

Three days in, only two people have applied — below expectation. It surfaces this itself and proposes two fixes: widen the JD from “backend” to “full-stack”, and put paid promotion behind the LinkedIn post. It asks permission before either.

Adaptation Human checkpoint approval for spend

Screening with judgement, not just filters

Applications pick up. It parses the résumés and reports a split: 2 strong candidates, 3 partial matches, 3 weak. Then it asks whether to schedule interviews with the two strong ones.

Tools résumé parser · Human checkpoint shortlist approval

Interview scheduling across calendars

It checks your calendar for free slots, proposes Friday, drafts the invitation email, and sends it to you and both candidates. On the day it reminds you and attaches a document of suggested interview questions.

Tools calendar API · email API · docs

Offer letter, on your word

You say one candidate impressed you. It pulls the company's letter template, drafts the offer, and asks you to review before anything leaves the building. On approval, it emails the candidate from your mailbox.

Human checkpoint review before send

Closing the loop into onboarding

It watches for acceptance, then reports: offer accepted, welcome email sent, IT access requested, laptop provisioned — shall I set up a meeting on the joining date?

Agent monitors · Human checkpoint final scheduling

The takeaway from the walkthrough

The most striking property is how autonomous the system is. You supplied a goal; it supplied the plan, the execution, and the recovery when execution went sideways. Your permissions are requested only at the sharp edges — posting publicly, spending money, rejecting people, sending offers.

04

The six characteristics

How do you tell whether a given system is genuinely agentic? These six traits are the test. Each one below is anchored back to the recruiter example.

03

04

1 · Autonomy

The biggest property — and the most dangerous one if left unmanaged

DefinitionAn AI system's ability to make decisions and take actions on its own to achieve a given goal, without needing step-by-step human instructions.

Autonomy is also proactiveness. Nothing in the recruiter example told the agent to check application volume — it was monitoring, noticed the numbers were low, and raised it before a human noticed. A generative chatbot would have waited for you to notice and ask.

Where autonomy shows up

  • Execution: it runs the plan's steps in order, by itself.
  • Decision-making: how many candidates to shortlist, and on what basis.
  • Tool usage: which tool to reach for at which step.
  • Recovery: what to do when a step fails.

Autonomy is not a single switch. It is present in every aspect of how the system works.

Four ways to control it

  • Scope permissions. “Screen all résumés, but ask me before rejecting anyone.”
  • Human-in-the-loop. Approval checkpoints before risky continuation — e.g. before posting a JD publicly.
  • Override controls. You can stop, pause or change behaviour at any time — “pause hiring” must actually halt the agent.
  • Guardrails & policies. Hard rules: never schedule interviews on weekends; never use informal language in emails.
Why control is non-negotiable — three failure modes: a fully autonomous agent could roll out job offers with the wrong salary and wrong terms; it could shortlist on nationality or age (illegal in many countries, and a real risk if the underlying model carries bias); or it could burn your ad budget on LinkedIn without asking. Autonomy unmanaged is not a feature, it is exposure.

2 · Goal orientation

The compass that gives autonomy a direction

DefinitionThe AI system operates with a persistent objective in mind and continuously directs its actions to achieve that objective, rather than just responding to isolated prompts.

Everything the recruiter agent did served one objective: hire a backend engineer. Autonomy says “I can act independently.” Goal orientation says “independently — in order to do what.” Without a goal, autonomy has nothing to steer by. The two traits move hand in hand.

Goals are given plainly, or with constraints

Independent goal

“Hire a backend engineer.” No conditions attached.

Goal with constraints

“Hire a backend engineer based in India, remote only, within $X budget.” Each clause narrows the search.

Goals live in core memory

The goal is not just a prompt you fired once — it is persisted. A rough conceptual shape (each framework stores it differently):

{
  "main_goal": "Hire a backend engineer",
  "constraints": {
    "experience": "2-4 years",
    "remote": true,
    "tech_stack": ["Python", "Django", "PostgreSQL"]
  },
  "status": "active",            // → "completed" at the end
  "created_at": "...",
  "progress": {
    "jd_created": true,
    "jd_posted": ["LinkedIn", "job board"],
    "applications": 8,
    "interviews_scheduled": 2,
    "hired": false,
    "onboarded": false
  }
}

Status tracking matters. When onboarded flips to true, the status moves to completed and the agent stops working. Until then, every new observation is checked against “where am I relative to the goal?”

Goals can be changed mid-flight. You post the backend role, wait seven days, get nothing, and decide to hire a freelancer for one project instead. The main goal changes, the plan is regenerated, and the agent starts executing the new plan. That flexibility is designed in, not accidental.

3 · Planning

Arguably the most important trait: how an agent actually works

DefinitionThe agent's ability to break a high-level goal into a structured sequence of actions and subgoals, and choose the best path to the desired outcome.

If someone asks how an agentic system works, the honest answer is two stages: plan, then execute. And the whole thing is iterative — execute a few steps, discover step four is impossible, go back, re-plan, continue. The loop runs until the goal is met.

Planning→Execution→observe → re-plan↺

Planning is a search problem, solved in three steps

STEP 1

Generate candidate plans

Not one plan — several. There are always multiple routes from the initial state (“we need an engineer”) to the final state (“we have one”).

STEP 2

Evaluate them

Score each candidate against criteria, then compare. This is where quality is decided.

STEP 3

Select the best

Keep one plan — either with human input, or via a pre-programmed policy inside the agent.

Two candidate plans for the same goal

PlanRouteWhere it wins / loses
A · Job portalsWrite JD, post to LinkedIn / GitHub Jobs / job boards, promote the post.Cheap and broad; slower to yield hires, volume can be noisy.
B · Referrals & agenciesRun an internal referral drive, or engage a hiring agency.Higher quality and speed; costs money, depends on network reach.

The five evaluation criteria

  • Efficiency — which plan executes faster?
  • Tool availability — if a plan needs a Google Search API you don't have, it is rejected automatically.
  • Cost — with a budget constraint, the cheap route wins.
  • Risk — which plan is most likely to fail?
  • Alignment with constraints — for remote hiring, will LinkedIn or internal referrals actually surface remote candidates?

You are not doing any of this comparison. The agent is. Your job is to set the goal, the constraints, and the approval points.

4 · Reasoning

The cognitive process that makes planning and execution possible at all

DefinitionThe cognitive process through which a system interprets information, draws conclusions, and makes decisions — both while planning and while executing.

A human analogy from the lecture: your phone gets stolen while travelling. You take in information from your environment (“my phone is gone”), interpret it, draw a conclusion (“the thief could misuse my number”), then decide (“call the carrier and block the SIM”). Interpret → conclude → decide. An agent does the same thing with the feedback its environment gives it.

Reasoning while planning

  • Goal decomposition — deciding what the steps even are is itself reasoning.
  • Tool selection — “to find salaries for 2–4 years experience I need an external search tool.”
  • Estimation — how long will this take, what does it depend on, what could go wrong.

Reasoning while executing

  • Choosing between options — three candidates match; interview two or all three?
  • Knowing when to ask — look up the salary band myself, or ask the human?
  • Error handling — LinkedIn's server is down: retry later, tell the human, or post elsewhere?

Why it is a first-class trait

Both stages of the agent loop — planning and execution — are saturated with moments that require judgement. Remove reasoning and you are left with a fixed script, which is just automation with extra steps.

5 · Adaptability

Changing the route, not the destination

DefinitionThe agent's ability to modify its plans, strategies and actions in response to unexpected conditions, while staying aligned with the goal.

Three days after posting, the recruiter agent noticed the application count was far below expectation. It did not wait to be told. It proposed adding paid promotion and widening the JD to full-stack. That is adaptability: the goal stayed fixed, the approach changed.

Three triggers for adaptation

1 · Failure

A tool or API dies. The calendar API is down, so it cannot see your availability — instead of failing, it messages you directly and asks.

2 · External feedback

The environment pushes back: too few applications, ad budget draining in two days. The signal comes from outside the agent, and it re-plans around it.

3 · Goal change

You switch from hiring an employee to contracting a freelancer. The objective itself moved, so the plan must be rebuilt from the top.

The tell: whenever an agentic system gets stuck, it finds some alternate path or alternate solution rather than stopping. If you see a system that only reports failure, it is not adaptable — it is just brittle.

6 · Context awareness

Why an agent can pick up a conversation days later

DefinitionThe agent's ability to understand, retain and utilise relevant information from the ongoing task, past interactions, user preferences and environmental cues — to make better decisions across a multi-step process.

Hiring runs for days. If the agent cannot retain context, day four is impossible: you ask “how many applicants so far?” and it has no idea what you are talking about. Context awareness is what makes long-running work coherent.

What context is actually held

Context typeIn the recruiter example
Original goal“Hire a backend engineer” — available at every moment, not just at the start.
Progress so farJD finalised and posted; 8 applications received; 2 interviews scheduled.
Conversation historyWhat you said about the role, what you approved, what you rejected.
Environment stateLive application count; the ad budget draining faster than expected.
Tool responses“Candidate B has 3 years of Django + AWS experience”; “the human is free at 2 pm”.
User preferencesThe company prefers remote candidates; the recruiter likes interview questions delivered as a Google Doc.
Policies & guardrailsNever send an offer letter without approval; never use platforms that require paid ads on your own initiative.

Implemented as memory: two layers

Short-term

This session

Current messages, tool calls, immediate decisions. “The résumé parser says candidate B has 3 years of experience.” Human equivalent: what you are working on right now, and the meeting you have at 4 pm.

Long-term

Across sessions

High-level goals, past interactions, preferences, decisions that must survive. “Never send an offer letter without asking.” Human equivalent: where you live, what you do for a living.

Memory gets its own deep-dive later in the playlist, when LangGraph is introduced. Here it is enough to know that context awareness is implemented by memory.

05

The five core components

Zoom out from behaviour to architecture. Nearly every agentic application is assembled from the same five parts — with a useful body analogy for each.

Brain — the LLM

The decision core

In an LLM-based agent (which is what this playlist covers), the brain is the model. It interprets what the user actually wants, decomposes the goal, reasons through planning and execution, picks which tool to call, and generates the natural language you talk to.

Body analogy: the brain. All the heavy lifting is here — it is the backbone of the workflow.

Orchestrator

The control flow

Takes the plan the brain produced and drives it step by step, routing on each result: step 1 → its output decides whether step 2, 3 or 4 runs next. Handles sequencing, conditional routing, retries, looping, iteration, and delegation of a task to a human or back to the LLM.

Body analogy: the nervous system — connected to every part. Or: the project manager. Built with frameworks such as LangGraph, CrewAI, or AutoGen.

Tools

The interface to the world

Every external action goes through a tool: an API call, a database write, an email, a calendar lookup, a résumé parser, an HR system. Even a RAG knowledge base counts as a tool — it retrieves factual, domain-specific information to ground the agent's responses.

Body analogy: hands and legs. Without them the agent can think but cannot do.

Memory

The retained state

Short-term memory holds the current session: user messages, tool calls, immediate decisions. Long-term memory holds high-level goals, past interactions, user preferences and cross-session decisions. Memory also carries state tracking — how much is done, how much remains.

Body analogy: lived experience. It is what turns a fresh session into a continuation.

Supervisor

The human-in-the-loop layer

Where the human and the agent work together. It notifies you before high-risk actions (sending an offer letter, running paid LinkedIn ads), takes your permission before proceeding, enforces guardrails, and handles escalations. Example escalation: a rule says hire only from IITs/NITs, but one candidate from elsewhere has an exceptional résumé — the supervisor alerts you rather than silently rejecting or silently hiring.

Body analogy: the manager who signs off. The component that makes autonomy governable.

This is a high-level map, not the full anatomy. Drill into any box and it subdivides — the “brain” alone contains a planner (generates candidate plans) and an evaluator (scores them). As you go deeper into LangGraph you will build more granular components. Five is the right altitude for a first pass.
06

Is it agentic? Six questions

Someone hands you a chatbot or an AI product and asks whether it is agentic. Ask these six. All yes = agentic.

Q1
Does it act without step-by-step instructions? Can it make decisions and take actions on its own toward the goal?
Q2
Does it hold a persistent objective? Or does it just answer isolated prompts, one at a time?
Q3
Does it plan? Can it decompose the goal into subgoals and ordered actions, and choose between candidate plans?
Q4
Does it reason? Does it interpret, conclude and decide — both while planning and while executing?
Q5
Does it adapt? When conditions change or a tool fails, does it find another route instead of stopping?
Q6
Does it retain context? Goal, progress, history, preferences, environment — across a multi-step, possibly multi-day task?
Quick self-test: which trait is missing?

“It answers well but I have to prompt every step.” — Not goal-oriented or autonomous. It is a reactive chatbot with good manners.

“It runs a fixed pipeline and crashes when an API fails.” — Has execution, lacks reasoning and adaptability. That is automation, not an agent.

“It forgets what we agreed yesterday.” — No context awareness, so no long-running task. Short-term memory only.

“It does everything, including things I never approved.” — Autonomous without a supervisor. Powerful, ungovernable, and the reason autonomy controls exist.

07

Recap, glossary & what's next

The lecture in five lines

  • Agentic AI takes a goal and owns the plan, the execution and the recovery.
  • It is proactive where generative AI is reactive.
  • Six traits: autonomy, goal orientation, planning, reasoning, adaptability, context awareness.
  • Autonomy must be governed — scope, checkpoints, overrides, guardrails.
  • Five components: brain, orchestrator, tools, memory, supervisor.

Mental model to keep

One loop, two stages, six traits, five parts.

The loop is goal → plan → act → observe → re-plan. The two stages are planning and execution. The six traits are what you test for. The five components are what you build.

Glossary

Agentic AI
An AI system that takes a goal and works toward it autonomously, with minimal human guidance.
Autonomy
Making decisions and taking actions without step-by-step instructions.
Human-in-the-loop
A deliberate checkpoint where human approval is required before the agent continues.
Guardrails
Hard rules and ethical boundaries the agent must never cross.
Candidate plans
Alternative plans generated for the same goal before one is selected.
Goal decomposition
Breaking one high-level objective into ordered subgoals and actions.
Environment
Everything the agent operates within and receives feedback from — a chessboard, a road, applicants on LinkedIn.
Orchestrator
The control-flow layer that sequences steps, routes on conditions, retries and delegates.
Short-term memory
Session-scoped information: messages, tool calls, immediate decisions.
Long-term memory
Cross-session state: goals, past interactions, preferences, standing rules.
Supervisor
The component implementing approvals, guardrail enforcement and escalation.
Escalation
Handing an edge case to a human rather than deciding it silently.
